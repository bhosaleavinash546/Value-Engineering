01 · What VE is

Value is function divided by cost.
You can improve either one.

Value engineering started at General Electric in 1947, when engineer Lawrence D. Miles noticed that the substitute materials used during wartime shortages often worked better and cost less. It’s now a structured way to improve the value of products, processes and projects by looking at what they do, and SAVE International publishes the most widely used standard for it (Europe also uses EN 12973).

VAVE

Value analysis and value engineering: the same method at two different times

Value analysis and value engineering are the same method. The only difference is when you use it. On products already in production, you take apart what you make today, question every function and part, and remove cost customers never asked to pay for. During design, you set function and cost targets before 70–80% of the cost is fixed, when the same effort saves much more. Same steps, same tools. Together they’re called VAVE.

⚙ On existing products

  • Start from today’s parts list and processes
  • Teardowns and should-cost comparisons
  • Quick payback, with changes made during production

✏ During design

  • Cost targets set for each part of the design
  • Choose concepts by what they need to do
  • Stop cost getting in, not just take it out

Four kinds of value · as described by Lawrence Miles

Use valueWhat the product does: the jobs it performs.
Esteem valueWhat makes people want it: brand, finish and how well made it seems.
Exchange valueWhat it’s worth to sell or trade: resale value and the price it can command.
Cost valueWhat it costs in total: material, labour, overhead and running costs over its life.

1947
The insight

Lawrence D. Miles creates Value Analysis at General Electric after wartime material substitutes prove better and cheaper.

1954
VE gets its name

The US Navy Bureau of Ships uses the method during design and names it “value engineering”.

1959
SAVE is founded

The Society of American Value Engineers is founded to set standards. It’s now called SAVE International.

1965
Japan adopts VE

The Society of Japanese Value Engineering is founded, and Japanese companies combine VE with target costing.

1993
Required by US policy

A US government rule (OMB Circular A-131) requires federal agencies to use VE. Europe later sets out value management in the EN 12973 standard.

2010s
Software arrives

Should-cost software, CT scanning and spend analysis make cost engineering much faster.

Today
AI and ongoing programmes

AI assistants, generative design and shared knowledge bases help companies run VE all the time, not as a one-off.

03 · FAQ

Common questions

What's the difference between Value Analysis, Value Engineering and VAVE?

Value analysis (VA) improves products that are already in production: you take them apart, question each part and redesign. Value engineering (VE) uses the same method during design, before the cost is locked in, which is when it saves the most. VAVE is the name for both together, and value management is the set of habits that keeps it going over time.

When should we run a VE study?

There are four usual reasons: a new product reaching its concept or design reviews (when VE saves the most), margins squeezed by price pressure or inflation, a teardown showing a competitor makes something for less, and, in companies that do this well, simply the calendar, with a VE round every year on each high-volume product.

Who should be in the workshop team?

Six to ten people from design, manufacturing, purchasing, quality, finance and service, plus key suppliers who know their own costs. A trained facilitator runs the process but doesn’t own the answers. A team from just one department tends to come up with the same kind of ideas, so the mix matters.

How long does a study take, end to end?

Two to four weeks to prepare (data, a costed parts list, samples and benchmarks), three to five days for the workshop itself covering all six phases, then six to twelve weeks to develop and implement the ideas. Quick wins can reach production within weeks; most savings arrive within three to six months.

How much does it actually save?

A first proper VE round on a product that’s never had one usually finds 8–15% of its cost. Companies that keep it going save 3–5% a year after that. A study usually returns more than 10 times what it costs, because the study is cheap compared with savings that repeat on every unit made.

Will cost reduction hurt our quality or brand?

Not if it’s done properly. VE is about improving value, so the functions customers need are kept or improved, and any feature you remove has to be backed by customer, warranty and usage data. Most quality problems come from flat percentage cuts, which is exactly what VE is meant to replace.

Which standards and certifications matter?

SAVE International’s Value Methodology Standard sets out the job plan used around the world, and EN 12973 covers value management in Europe. Practitioners can certify through SAVE as VMA (associate) and CVS (certified value specialist). In the UK, the Institute of Value Management awards QVA and PVM under the European scheme based on EN 12973. A study led by a CVS is seen as the professional standard.

How is VE different from ordinary cost-cutting?

Cost-cutting starts from the accounts and removes whatever is easiest, which is often something the customer valued. VE starts from what the product needs to do and what that’s worth, uses should-cost data to find where you’re overpaying, brings different departments together to find alternatives, and only then takes cost out. Cost-cutting makes the product smaller. VE makes it better value.
