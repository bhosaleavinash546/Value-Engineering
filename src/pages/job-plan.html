The six steps of a VE study

This is the standard way to run a value engineering study, as set out by SAVE International. It has six steps that always run in the same order, with preparation before the workshop and follow-up afterwards to make sure the savings actually happen.

Why it pays to start early

Most of a product’s cost is decided long before the money is actually spent, because early design choices lock it in. The gap between the two lines is where value engineering can make the biggest difference.

  1. Concept and design
  2. Testing and tooling
  3. Production

About 70–80% of a product’s cost is fixed by the time the design is finished, while less than 10% has been spent. The exact share is debated, so treat it as a rule of thumb. That’s why VE work done during design pays back many times more than the same work after launch.

Phase 1 Information: get the facts first

Before anyone suggests an idea, gather everything you need to know. That means the scope and limits of the study, what customers and the market need, quality and warranty history, volumes, and a full cost breakdown of every part, down to material, process, labour and overhead. Go and see the production line and handle the parts. Then ask Lawrence Miles’ five questions: What is it? What does it do? What does it cost? What is it worth? What else could do the job?

Key activities

  • Agree the scope and the savings target
  • Break down the cost and find the biggest items (Pareto)
  • Capture what customers need (voice of the customer)
  • Visit the line and suppliers, and collect samples
  • Buy competitor products to take apart

Tools

  • Cost models and spend analysis
  • Pareto (ABC) analysis
  • Quality function deployment (QFD) and the Kano model
  • Process flow maps
Deliverable

A cost and requirements baseline that everyone on the team agrees on. The whole study builds on it.

~20%of the workshop time. Don’t skip it.

Phase 2 Function Analysis: the core of VE

This is what makes VE different from ordinary cost-cutting. Describe each part of the product by what it does, in two words: a verb and a noun, such as “transmit torque”, “resist corrosion” or “convey esteem”. Decide whether each function is basic (the reason the product exists) or secondary (just how this design happens to work). Lay the functions out in a FAST diagram, where reading right answers “how?” and reading left answers “why?”. Then build a function–cost matrix that shares out every pound of cost across the functions it pays for.

Key activities

  • List every function as a verb and a noun
  • Sort them into basic and secondary
  • Draw a FAST diagram with scope lines
  • Share out the cost and estimate the worth of each function
  • Work out the Value Index (cost ÷ worth) for each one

Tools

  • FAST diagram (technical or customer)
  • Function–cost matrix
  • A ranking of the biggest cost–worth gaps
  • Worth estimate (the lowest cost that does the job)

Phase 3 Creative: more ideas means better ideas

Come up with as many ways as possible to deliver each target function, rather than small tweaks to the current design. Nobody judges ideas at this stage, and wild ideas are welcome because they open up new options. A good workshop produces 200–400 ideas using techniques such as brainstorming, brainwriting, TRIZ, SCAMPER and analogy. See all twelve techniques →.

Key activities

  • Generate ideas one function at a time
  • Look at how other industries do the same job
  • Collect ideas from suppliers and the shop floor
  • Record each idea with an owner and the function it targets

Ground rules

  • Defer all judgement
  • Quantity over quality
  • Build on others' ideas ("yes, and…")
  • Question functions, not people or departments
Deliverable

A list of ideas, each linked to the function and cost it targets, ready to be evaluated.

200–400ideas from a good cross-functional team

Phase 4 Evaluation: pick the ideas worth pursuing

Cut hundreds of ideas down to a shortlist worth the engineering time. Start with a quick sort into go, grow or park. Then score the rest on savings, technical risk, investment, time to implement and effect on the customer. Group ideas that work well together into scenarios, and give each one a champion who will take it forward.

Key activities

  • Sort ideas into go, grow or park
  • Score them against weighted criteria
  • Compare concepts with the current design (Pugh method)
  • Group ideas that work together into scenarios
  • Rank them by effort and impact

Tools

  • Pugh matrix
  • Weighted evaluation matrix
  • Effort–impact chart
  • Early risk check, before a full failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA)
Deliverable

A ranked shortlist, usually the best 10–15% of ideas, each with an owner, an estimated saving and a risk rating.

≈ 1 in 8raw ideas makes it into development

Phase 5 Development: turn ideas into business cases

Each champion turns their idea into a proposal that can actually be implemented. That includes engineering checks (simulation and test plans), supplier quotes or should-cost estimates, tooling and investment, any quality or regulatory impact, and a before-and-after cost comparison. Every proposal should answer five questions: what changes, what it saves, what it costs to do, what could go wrong, and when it will happen.

Key activities

  • Check it works, with calculations and simulation
  • Should-cost estimate and supplier quotes
  • Design FMEA and a test plan
  • One-off cost and payback
  • Find out what re-approval the change needs (PPAP in automotive, for example)

Tools

  • Cleansheet cost models
  • Design and process FMEA
  • Business case template
  • Tolerance and simulation (CAE) studies
Deliverable

Proposals ready for a decision, with net savings, one-off cost, payback and a dated plan.

< 12 mopayback target for most changes to existing products

Phase 6 Presentation: get the decision, then deliver

Present the proposals to the people who decide: the reasoning, the numbers, the risks and what you need from them. Get a clear yes or no on each one, plus the resources to do it, then hand over to implementation with named owners and milestones. The work only counts when the savings show up in the accounts, so check implemented ideas against the baseline, and feed what you learned back into your design rules and cost standards.

Key activities

  • Present to the decision makers and keep a decision log
  • An implementation plan with owners and dates
  • Track each idea through the savings funnel to L4
  • Check implemented savings against the starting cost
  • Add what you learned to the design guidelines

Tools

  • Savings funnel tracker
  • Stage-gate change process
  • Tracking IDs that link each idea to the accounts
Deliverable

Approved proposals being implemented, a savings funnel that’s kept up to date, and lessons recorded for the next study.
