4.1 · More than cost

  • Lower cost. A first proper round on a product nobody has studied usually finds 8–15% of product cost. Established programmes keep finding 3–5% a year, and studies often return more than 10 times what they cost to run.
  • Better quality. Fewer parts means fewer things to go wrong. Every part removed is one that can't fail, be fitted wrongly or run out.
  • Simpler, faster manufacturing. Fewer steps and less equipment, so there's less to maintain and quicker changeovers.
  • Better supplier relationships. Suppliers work with you on the design, instead of arguing with you about price every year.
  • Less material and CO₂. Lighter designs with fewer parts lower cost and emissions at the same time.
  • Stronger teams. People learn to think in functions and work across departments. The skill stays after the project ends.

Sources: 8–15% and 3–5% are typical practitioner ranges; published results vary widely, from about 5% to over 40%, depending on the product. "More than 10 times": US state highway VE programmes report returns of 41:1 to over 75:1 (Virginia and Minnesota DOTs).

4.2 · Proof at scale

Case study — documented

$1.7bnsaved a year, on average1,224changes made in 2011

US highway programme. US law requires a VE study on large highway projects. From 2002 to 2011, the studies delivered an average of $1.7 billion a year in savings that were actually put into practice. In 2011 alone, 378 studies led to 1,224 changes, worth about $1.0 billion. People believe the numbers because every recommendation is tracked and published. Source: FHWA Value Engineering annual summary reports.

Case study — documented

152 → 32parts−91%assembly time

IBM Proprinter. In the 1980s, IBM designed its printer for easy assembly. Parts went from 152 to 32 and assembly time fell by 91%, with every screw replaced by snap-fits. Source: Boothroyd Dewhurst Inc., DFMA case history.

Case study — documented

~70 → 1–2parts, as large castings~40%lower cost for that assembly~30%lighter

Tesla Model Y. About 70 pressed and welded parts were replaced with one or two large castings. Reports put the saving at about 40% of that assembly's manufacturing cost, with hundreds of welding robots no longer needed and a rear body about 30% lighter. There were new risks (repairs and casting quality) that had to be tested before the savings were real. Sources: Tesla statements; industry reporting.

4.3 · Reading a VAVE business case

A good proposal answers five questions:

  • What exactly changes?
  • What does it save? (checked by finance)
  • What does it cost to bring in? (tooling, testing, engineering time)
  • What could go wrong, and how will it be handled?
  • When does it happen?

Worked example

Replace a machined bracket with a cheaper stamped one (made by fine blanking). The saving is 30p per unit × 300,000 units = £90,000 a year. The one-time cost is £38,000 for tooling plus £7,000 for testing, £45,000 in total. Payback is 6 months.

One detail leaders often miss: savings start on the day the change goes into production, not the day it's approved. If a decision in this example is delayed by six months, that delay costs £45,000.

4.4 · VAVE in the AI era

AI doesn't change what VAVE is. It changes how fast teams can do it. Four uses matter most today:

  • Should-cost in minutes. Costing software reads a product's 3D model, works out how it would be made, and estimates its cost in different countries. It takes minutes instead of the weeks it takes to get supplier quotes. One leading tool covers more than 90 regions of the world.
  • Finding hidden duplication. AI can scan thousands of parts and purchase records to find near-identical parts, and the same part bought at different prices.
  • More ideas, faster. AI language tools suggest ideas for each function and summarise warranty and customer comments, so the workshop starts from better information.
  • Better designs from the start. Generative design software tries thousands of shapes to find the lightest one that is still strong enough.

What AI doesn't change: people still decide what customers value, check every number and test every change. AI gives you a first draft, not the answer.

Two rules for leaders: keep confidential cost and supplier data out of public AI tools, and never approve a saving based only on an AI estimate.

Case study — documented

8 → 1parts40%lighter20%stronger

GM and Autodesk. Using generative design, GM engineers redesigned a seat bracket: eight parts became one 3D-printed part that was 40% lighter and 20% stronger. It was a proof of concept rather than a production part, but it shows AI delivering two classic VAVE results at once: fewer parts and less material. Source: GM and Autodesk, 2018; reported by Green Car Congress and CompositesWorld.

Source for "more than 90 regions": aPriori, product documentation.

Key points for leaders

  • Expect 8–15% from a first proper study, and 3–5% a year once VAVE is how you work.
  • Count the other benefits too: quality, speed, supplier relationships, CO₂ and skills.
  • Ask the five questions of every proposal, and remember that delay has a price.
  • AI makes VAVE faster, not automatic: people still decide, check and test.
