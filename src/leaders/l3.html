How a VAVE study runs

You will learn

  • What happens before, during and after a VAVE workshop
  • Who needs to be in the room
  • How savings are tracked until they're real

3.1 · Before the workshop: 2–4 weeks

Most failed studies fail before they start. Preparation needs three things:

  • A sponsor: someone senior enough to provide people and money, and to act on the decisions. This is usually you.
  • A written scope: which product, its starting cost, the target (usually 10–20% of the cost being studied) and what's off limits.
  • An information pack: a costed parts list checked by finance, quality and warranty history, what customers actually need, and ideally a teardown of a competitor's product and a should-cost estimate.

Without a scope, a study drifts. Without a sponsor, it produces slides and nothing else.

3.2 · Who's in the room

Six to ten people from different departments: design, manufacturing, purchasing, quality, finance and service, plus key suppliers who know their materials and parts. A trained facilitator runs the process but doesn't supply the answers.

A team from one department comes up with the same kind of ideas. The best ideas come when people from different departments challenge each other.

3.3 · The workshop week: six steps

  • 1 · Information Day 1: Go through the costs, walk the production line, look at the product.
  • 2 · Function analysis Days 1–2: Map what each part does and what each function costs, then find the mismatches.
  • 3 · Creative Day 3: A full day of ideas for the costly functions. No criticism allowed.
  • 4 · Evaluation Day 4: Sort, screen and score the ideas. Each surviving idea gets a named owner.
  • 5 · Development Day 5: Turn the best ideas into business cases, with risks noted.
  • 6 · Presentation Day 5: A decision meeting with the sponsor: yes, no or "more data by a date" for every idea.

Smaller studies can run in three days. The one thing you can't shorten is keeping the steps apart. If you come up with ideas and judge them in the same session, both suffer.

3.4 · After the workshop: where savings are won or lost

Approved ideas go into a savings funnel:

  • L1 Idea
  • L2 Validated: finance has checked the savings
  • L3 Approved
  • L4 Implemented: the change is in production

The funnel is reviewed every month. Every idea has an owner, a stage and a date, or it's dropped. Savings only count at L4, measured against the starting cost that was fixed before the study, so price rises and changes in product mix can't blur the result.

This is the step organisations most often neglect: 40–60% of approved ideas never reach production. Simple changes can go live within weeks. Changes that need new tooling or testing take months, and they need someone senior watching them.

3.5 · What about risk?

The first worry most leaders have is that cutting cost will hurt quality or safety. In a well-run study it can't, because every change goes through the same checks as any other design change:

  • Tested before approval: prototypes, simulation or trials prove the change meets every requirement.
  • Approved by the right people: quality and engineering sign off, and so does the customer where contracts require it. In the car industry, for example, that's the Production Part Approval Process (PPAP).
  • Brought in under change control: with a date, an owner and a way back if something goes wrong.

An idea that can't pass these checks isn't a saving. It's a risk, and it's rejected.

What it asks of you

  • Before: an hour to agree the scope and target, and to name the team.
  • During: open the workshop, then chair the decision meeting at the end (about two hours).
  • After: 30 minutes a month reviewing the savings funnel, and quick decisions when something is blocked.

Key points for leaders

  • Before a study starts, make sure it has a sponsor, a scope and a target. If it doesn't, don't start it.
  • Give the study a mixed team, and protect their time for the whole week.
  • Judge VAVE on savings actually in production (L4), not on the list of ideas in the final presentation.
