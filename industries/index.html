Guide 07 of 10

VE in eight industries

The method works the same way everywhere, but the biggest savings come from different places in each industry. For each of eight industries you’ll find a typical cost breakdown, the approaches that save the most, and realistic savings ranges.

By Avinash Bhosale · Updated 25 September 2026 · 7 min read

In 30 seconds

The method is the same in every industry, but what saves the most money isn’t. For eight industries, this page shows where the cost usually sits, which approaches save the most, and the one move that stands out. In cars, for example, 60–75% of the cost is in parts bought from suppliers, so sharing parts across models and working closely with suppliers pay off most. In consumer goods, it’s packaging, recipes and cutting the number of product variants.

Automotive & Electric Vehicles

Carmakers watch costs more closely than almost any other industry. Between 60% and 75% of a vehicle’s cost is in parts bought from suppliers, spread across thousands of parts and hundreds of suppliers, and customers expect prices to come down every year. Electric vehicles change the picture: the battery alone is 30–40% of an EV’s cost, so battery cells, pack design and power electronics are now where the biggest savings are.

Signature move

Large single castings replace around 70 stamped and welded body parts with one or two aluminium castings. That removes hundreds of robots, joints and tolerance problems from the body shop.

3–8%reduction in product cost each year, kept up through yearly VE rounds and supplier programmes
30–40%of an EV’s cost is the battery, the single biggest target for VE

Consumer Products & FMCG

Huge volumes, thin margins and tough shelf prices mean a tenth of a penny per unit matters when you sell hundreds of millions. Most of the cost is in materials, packaging and the product recipe, and having too many product variants adds cost everywhere. VE here has to protect what the customer experiences, so any cost you take out should go unnoticed.

Highest-yield levers

Signature move

Making bottles lighter by taking grams out of the plastic preforms and caps across a billion bottles. Shoppers don’t notice, but it makes a big difference to profit and to the company’s environmental figures.

5–12%reduction in cost of goods from combined packaging, recipe and complexity work
20–30%of product variants usually bring in less than 2% of margin, so they’re the first to review

Heavy Machinery & Construction / Agricultural Equipment

Low volumes, lots of variety and heavy steel. Most of the cost is in fabricated structures, castings, hydraulics and the powertrain. Cost control is hard because every order seems to be custom, so the biggest savings come from the product architecture: build products from standard modules and configure each order instead of engineering it from scratch. Every kilo of steel, every weld and every variant costs money.

Signature move

Replacing welded assemblies made of many pieces with a single ductile-iron casting, once volumes are high enough. There are fewer welds, less machining and less distortion, and it costs 20–40% less at the right yearly quantity.

8–15%unit cost reduction that’s typical in a first structured VE programme
30–50%fewer engineered variants when products are built from standard modules

Home Appliances

Appliances are where VE is used most. Fridges, washing machines, air conditioners and hobs combine sheet metal, motors or compressors, plastics, foam and more and more electronics, and they sell at very tight retail prices. The companies that do best use platforms: one family of motors, one family of control boards and one cabinet design shared across many brands and price levels.

Highest-yield levers

Motor / compressor platformingSheet-metal yield & gauge optimisationUniversal parts across brandsElectronics integration (one-board design)Insulation & foam optimisationTeardown benchmarking vs. Asian OEMsPackaging & cube optimisationEnergy-rating cost balance
Signature move

One inverter motor design used across washing machines, dryers and dishwashers. That triples the volume on one design, with one set of supplier tooling and one quality record, and cuts motor cost by double digits.

6–12%unit cost reduction from a VE and platform programme across the whole range
#1teardown benchmarking is the appliance industry’s most established VE practice

Aerospace & Defence

A kilogram of aircraft can cost hundreds of pounds, certification can lock a design for decades, and with titanium buy-to-fly ratios of 8:1, most of the metal you buy ends up as swarf. VE has to work within what’s already certified: blanks closer to the final shape, different manufacturing processes and fewer assembly hours all save money without reopening type certificates. On new aircraft, 3D printing opens up new options.

Highest-yield levers

Buy-to-fly reduction (near-net blanks)AM part consolidationAssembly-hour reduction & determinate assemblyTitanium chip reversionShould-cost on sole-source suppliersStandard fastener rationalisationComposite vs. metallic trade studiesObsolescence & LTB management
Signature move

3D printing brackets and fuel-system parts as single pieces. Twenty parts that were machined and joined become one printed part that is lighter and cheaper, with fewer places to leak and fewer inspections.

5–10%reduction in ongoing costs without changing certified performance
8:1 → 2:1improvement in buy-to-fly ratio possible with near-net-shape blanks

Electronics & High-Tech

Costs change faster here than in any other industry. Component prices fall every quarter, chips keep absorbing more functions so parts disappear, and contract manufacturers price assembly by the second. VE is a regular routine rather than a yearly event: go through the parts list every quarter, qualify alternative parts and review designs with your contract manufacturer.

Cost structure (typical share of unit cost)

Semiconductors & actives
~40%
Passives, PCB & mechanicals
~25%
Display / battery / modules
~20%
EMS assembly & test
~15%

Highest-yield levers

Silicon integration (SoC / SiP)PCB layer & area reductionAlternate-source qualificationQuarterly BOM cost scrubsDFT & test-time reductionEMS should-cost & quote levellingConnector & cable rationalisationReference-design adoption
Signature move

Each board redesign moves more separate functions into a single chip or chip package. Every redesign removes dozens of components, makes the board smaller and cuts placement and test time, building up to 10–20% savings on the parts list.

10–20%reduction in parts cost with each major redesign of a mature product
Quarterlyhow often the leading companies review their parts costs

Medical Devices

Every change has regulatory consequences: design history files, biocompatibility, sterilisation validation, and whether a new 510(k) or CE submission is needed. The approach that works is VE that plans around regulation: group changes into planned submissions, prefer changes that only need to be documented on file, and design cost out of the next generation using what you learned from tearing down the current one.

Cost structure (typical share of unit cost)

Materials & components
~35%
Conversion & cleanroom labour
~25%
Quality, regulatory & sterilisation
~20%
Packaging & logistics

Highest-yield levers

Resin & material substitution (with biocomp strategy)Sterile-packaging optimisationDesign for sterilisation methodComponent standardisation across familiesSupplier consolidation & dual sourcingAutomation of cleanroom assemblyNext-gen design-to-costInstrument reprocessing economics
Signature move

Redesigning the sterile packaging, with trays sized correctly and one pouch layer instead of two, validated once for a whole product family. It regularly saves 20–30% of packaging cost with no change the patient would notice.

5–10%reduction in cost of goods while staying compliant
2×the savings when VE targets the next generation instead of changing the current one

Industrial Equipment & Energy

Motors, drives, transformers, switchgear, pumps, compressors, and solar and wind equipment. Copper, steel and electrical-grade laminations make up most of the cost, and efficiency regulations set minimum standards. VE finds the best balance of copper, iron and performance, uses the same frames across different power ratings, and looks at total cost of ownership, because customers are paying for a lifetime of energy use, not just the machine.

Cost structure (typical share of unit cost)

Copper, steel & magnetics

~18%

Highest-yield levers

Electromagnetic design optimisation (Cu ↔ Fe trade)Aluminium-for-copper windings where validFrame & platform standardisationRaw-material indexation & hedgingLamination nesting & yieldPower-electronics integrationDesign for field serviceTCO-based product positioning
Signature move

Redesigning a motor family’s electromagnetics for today’s copper and steel prices. The best balance of copper and steel changes with commodity prices, so older designs slowly drift away from it without anyone noticing.

8–15%reduction in material cost from electromagnetic redesign and standard frames
40%+of unit cost depends on commodity metal prices, so linking prices to an index is essential
