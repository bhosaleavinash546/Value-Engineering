Teardown and benchmarking

The best way to find out what a function should cost is to look at a competitor who already does it for less. This guide shows how to take their product apart and learn from it.

By Avinash Bhosale · Updated 25 September 2026 · 2 min read

In 30 seconds

Taking a competitor’s product apart is the most reliable way to find out what a function should cost. There are five steps. Photograph and weigh everything before you touch it. Take it apart one level at a time and time each step. Record every part. Estimate what each part costs the competitor to make. Then turn what you found into ideas for your own product. Look beyond price, too. This page covers six ways to compare.

What a teardown looks like, in two stages

This is how a teardown team sees a vehicle. First it comes apart into nine main sections, then each of those opens up into parts you can count, weigh and cost. Just scroll: the vehicle comes apart as it reaches the middle of your screen, and goes back together as you scroll on.

1

Buy and record

Buy the competitor’s products, then photograph, weigh and measure everything before you undo a single screw.

2

Take it apart step by step

Take it apart one level at a time and time each step. Reversed, the way it comes apart shows how much it costs them to assemble.

3

Record every part

Log every part: its material, weight, how it was made, supplier markings, clues about the tooling, and how many fasteners it uses.

4

Estimate each part’s cost

Work out what each part should cost the competitor to make. Comparing line by line shows exactly where your costs are higher.

5

Turn findings into ideas

Compare your product and theirs function by function, and feed the results into the creative phase. For each good idea, decide whether to copy it, adapt it or go one better.

Six ways to compare products

Cost benchmarkingCompare what each part should cost with competitors and the best factories.
Functional benchmarkingCompare how much performance you get for the money, such as torque, light output or capacity per pound, rather than the raw figures.
Design benchmarkingCompare how products are put together: the layout, how many parts are combined, the materials used and how parts are fastened.
Process benchmarkingCompare how the best factories make it: how much is automated, cycle times, yield and layout.
Feature benchmarkingCompare which features competitors offer at each price point, to see who gives away too much and who charges too little.
Patent & IP benchmarkingLook through patents for expired ones you’re free to use, and current ones you’ll need to design around.

Documented case

A teardown that changed what carmakers thought was possible. In 2024, the engineering firm Caresoft took apart BYD’s Seagull, a city car selling for around $12,000. Western carmakers studied the results closely: the car was profitable and used far fewer parts than they expected. In 2020, Munro’s public teardown of the Tesla Model Y did something similar, showing the whole industry Tesla’s large one-piece rear casting. Any product on sale can be studied this way, and the best companies do it.
