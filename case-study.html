The classroom projector: a complete value study

          Get the facts before the ideas

          Before the workshop

          • Pick the project. The VH-3600 is the highest-volume model and its cost is under pressure from cheaper rivals.
          • Name a sponsor and a team: the product director as sponsor; optics, electronics and mechanical engineers, purchasing, the factory, quality, service, product marketing and finance, with a facilitator.
          • Set the scope: the whole projector, its packaging and the factory process, up to delivery in the UK. The brand owner's own overheads and the sales channel are out of scope.
          • Ask for data in advance: a should-cost report with a teardown, the factory audit, tooling, volumes, warranty data and customer interviews.

          The brief

          Product
          VH-3600 classroom projector, 200,000 a year, made by a contract manufacturer in Shenzhen
          Goal
          Take at least 10% (£14.27) off the landed cost of £142.70, with no loss of brightness (3,600 lumens white and colour), picture, reliability or safety, at the same £425 price
          And
          Lower what it costs a school to own one
          Decision
          The sponsor decides in week 6

          The facts gathered

          • Where the cost sits (section B): the optical engine is £55.20, 53% of the parts. Electronics £30.10, case and cooling £12.80, packing and accessories £5.90. Making it adds £27.60 and getting it to the UK £9.90.
          • The market: £425 in the shops (£354.17 ex VAT). After the £141.50 landed cost, the brand owner and the shops share about 60% of the ex-VAT price.
          • The factory: 42 people and 85 minutes of work a projector, most of it waiting in burn-in. The panel alignment rig is the most expensive step.
          • The customer: interviews with IT managers and teachers, sorted with the Kano model below.

          Checking the data

          Even a good report needs checking before a team builds on it. Every total in it adds up, and every wave and saving in the roll-up matches its ideas. Five things didn't, and the team noted them before going on:

          1. Tooling: the report amortises £880k, but the tools it lists come to £715k. The other £165k (perhaps fixtures and alignment rigs) isn't itemised.
          2. The factory margin is described as 8% of £122.20, which would be £9.78. The report uses £9.40, which is 7.7%. The team keeps £9.40.
          3. VAVE-10 is headed £1.55 a projector, but the roll-up counts £1.85, including £0.30 of freight. The team uses £1.85, as the roll-up does.
          4. Panel alignment is £3.10 in the text and the DLP comparison, but £2.00 in the factory table. The table is used for the process; the comparison is left as the report has it.
          5. Parts the report doesn't list: its idea VAVE-19 saves 28p on printed manuals that aren't in its bill of materials. That and four other small parts are added as lines X1–X5 (£1.20), which is why the landed cost here is £142.70, not £141.50.

          What schools value (Kano)

          Must-be

          Safe. Reliable. A bright, readable picture in a lit classroom, in colour as well as white. Works with any laptop over HDMI.

          Missing one of these loses the sale.
          More is better

          Brightness. Lamp life. Low running cost. A quiet fan. Quick to set up.

          Each step up wins more buyers.
          Delighters

          Instant on. Wireless casting. Automatic keystone.

          Unexpected, and remembered.
          Indifferent for most

          A second VGA input and VGA out. RS-232C. A VGA cable in the box (over 90% connect by HDMI). A thick printed manual.

          Costs money, adds little. Worth testing.

          2Phase 2 · Function analysis

          What does it do, and what should each job cost?

          Function analysis is the heart of value engineering. Customers don't pay for parts; they pay for what the parts do. This step turns the projector into a set of functions, maps how they depend on each other, puts a cost and a worth on each one, and shows where money is spent on things the customer doesn't value. It's done in seven steps.

          1. 1Name the functions
          2. 2Classify them
          3. 3Build the FAST diagram
          4. 4Cost the functions
          5. 5Find where the cost sits
          6. 6Set the worth
          7. 7Pick the targets

          2.1Name the functions

          Why this step: you can't improve what you can't describe. Naming each job in two words makes the team agree what every part is for, and frees them from how it's done today.

          The rules

          • An active verb and a measurable noun. Generate light (lumens), Remove heat (watts), Conduct current (amps).
          • Say what it does, not how. The reflector's job is to collect light; reflecting is only how it does it.
          • Avoid weak verbs such as provide, allow, enable, permit and ensure. They hide the real job.
          • Avoid nouns you can't measure, such as user, buyer or quality.
          • Name the job, not the part. Trap dust, not "filter".

          Wrong and right

          Examples of weak function names and better ones
          PartWeakBetter
          LampProvide lightGenerate light
          Lamp doorAllow accessExpose lamp
          Interlock switchProtect userInterrupt power
          Front bezelLook niceEnhance appearance
          Air filterFilterTrap dust
          Tilt footLevel imageAdjust angle
          CartonProtect productAbsorb shock

          Common mistake: describing the part or the way it works. A good function would still be true if the part were replaced by something completely different.

          2.2Classify them

          Why this step: it separates the job the customer pays for from the jobs that only support it, protect it or clean up after it. That's where unnecessary cost usually hides.

          Basic

          The reason the product exists. One per product: Project image.

          Secondary, required

          Needed to deliver the basic function the way it's designed: Generate light, Remove heat.

          Unwanted

          A side effect nobody asked for: Generate heat, Emit UV, Generate interference, Generate noise, Consume energy. Worth £0.

          Esteem

          Makes the product appealing rather than making it work: Enhance appearance. Use functions make it work; esteem functions make people want it.

          Common mistake: treating every function as essential. In most products, more of the cost sits in secondary functions than in the basic one.

          2.3Build the FAST diagram

          Why this step: the FAST diagram (Function Analysis System Technique) turns a list of functions into logic. It shows which functions lie on the path to the customer's need and which hang off it, and it tests the team's understanding of the product.

          How to read it

          • Higher-order function (left, outside scope): why the customer wants the product. Communicate information.
          • Basic function (first inside scope): Project image.
          • Critical path: each step to the right answers how?, each step to the left answers why?
          • Scope lines mark what the study covers. Supply power, from the building's mains, is the input outside scope.
          • AND: two or more boxes stacked under one function are all needed together. Modulate light needs Polarise light and Separate wavelengths and Homogenise light.
          • Below the path: functions that happen when a path function happens, or are caused by it. Unwanted ones are in red.
          • Above the path: functions needed all the time, one-time functions, user functions and esteem.
          • Design criteria (dashed box) are requirements such as brightness, noise and safety, not functions. The objective sits beside them so nobody forgets what the study is for.

          How the team built it

          1. Write every function on a card.
          2. Find the basic function: the one the customer pays for.
          3. Ask "how?" again and again to build the critical path to the right.
          4. Check it backwards with "why?". If a step doesn't make sense both ways, a card is in the wrong place.
          5. Draw the scope lines.
          6. Place the "when" and "caused by" functions below, including the unwanted ones.
          7. Place the all-time, one-time, user and esteem functions above.
          8. Add the design criteria, then test the whole diagram out loud.

          Try it: how do we project an image? By magnifying it. How do we magnify it? We need one full-colour image, so we combine images. Now read it back: why combine images? So we can magnify one image. Why magnify it? To project it.

          The VH-3600 FAST diagram

          Click a box to see its parts in 3D. Switch on the costs to see where the money goes.

          Common mistake: laying the cards out in the order the parts sit in the product rather than in how–why order, or letting the diagram grow past the scope.

          2.4Cost the functions

          Why this step: a part can do several jobs, and a job can need several parts. Sharing each part's cost out among its functions shows what every job really costs. A parts list never can.

          Each share is estimated from what the part contains. The main board's video chip, panel drivers and memory process the signal (£13.00); its network interface receives it (£0.55); its amplifier drives the speaker (£0.45). The shares always add back up to the part's cost.

          Cost basis: the parts cost of one projector, £105.20, at 200,000 a year. Assembly, overheads and freight aren't shared out over functions; they're handled with the factory ideas in phase 3.

          Where the cost goes on the FAST diagram

          Common mistake: putting a part's whole cost on its main function. The main board would then hide what receiving the signal and sound really cost.

          2.5Find where the cost sits

          Why this step: a few functions carry most of the cost. Ranking them shows where effort will pay back, and stops the team spending days on functions worth pennies.

          Function costs, largest first

          2.6Set the worth

          Why this step: cost tells you what you pay. Worth tells you what you should pay: the lowest cost shown to perform the function reliably. The difference is the prize.

          Five ways to set worth

          • A cheaper alternative that does the same job to the same standard: organic polarisers where it runs cooler.
          • A supplier quote or should-cost: local optical glass.
          • A benchmark, but only if it meets every must-be need. DLP is cheaper, but fails on colour brightness, so it can't set a worth.
          • What the customer needs: if only part of a function is used, only that part has worth (one VGA input, not three).
          • The theoretical minimum: unwanted functions are worth £0; a one-time job is worth the least that does it once.

          Two numbers from cost and worth

          • Value gap = cost − worth. The money at stake.
          • Value index = cost ÷ worth. 1.0 is fair value. Above about 1.25 is worth a look; 2 or more is a strong target, a common rule of thumb.
          • Use both. A high index on a few pence matters less than a modest index on many pounds.

          What the school values: a paired comparison

          Worth asks "what's the cheapest way to do this?". The team also asks "how much does the school care?". Each pair of functions the school can see is compared, and the winner gets 1, 2 or 3 points. This is the study team's judgement, made on the school's behalf from the interviews in phase 1, and it isn't perfectly consistent: three of the low-scoring functions beat each other in a circle, which happens in real workshops.

          Importance against cost: the value graph

          Where a function's share of the cost is far bigger than its importance, look harder. Process signal and Enclose components are flagged here and confirmed by the worth evidence. Combine images is flagged too, but customers can't see what it does and there's no cheaper way to do it, so it stays. Modulate light costs more than its share of importance, but nothing cheaper meets the must-be needs. Use the graph to find questions; use worth to answer them.

          Common mistake: setting worth from a benchmark that doesn't meet the must-be needs, or from an idea nobody has checked.

          3Phase 3 · Creative

          Lots of ideas, no judging yet

          The team works on the target functions only, using brainstorming, SCAMPER, TRIZ, design for manufacture and assembly, the teardown and ideas from suppliers. The rule is simple: no idea is criticised during the session. Twenty-four ideas come out: the report's twenty (V01–V20) and four more (H1–H4). Each one is tied to the functions and parts it changes; press 3D to see them in the model. A quick screen afterwards drops anything that breaks a must-be need, and parks anything that's really a different product.

          Gate check before phase 4
          • Ideas for every target function
          • Nothing judged during the session
          • Only must-be failures dropped at screening, with the reason written down

          4Phase 4 · Evaluation

          Score every idea the same way

          Before anyone scores an idea, the team and the sponsor agree the criteria and their weights: saving 30%, risk 25%, what the customer gets 20%, one-off cost 15% and speed 10%. Each idea is then scored from 1 to 5 against each one, where 5 is always good: a big saving, a low risk, a small investment. Agreeing the weights first stops people working back from the answer they want. Eighteen ideas are a clear go; three need a feasibility check first.

          Gate check before phase 5
          • Criteria and weights agreed before scoring
          • Every score has a reason
          • Shortlist agreed by the sponsor

          5Phase 5 · Development

          Turn the best ideas into business cases

          A good idea isn't a saving yet. Each idea on the shortlist gets numbers finance will sign: the saving per projector, the one-off cost, the payback, the risks and the tests needed before it goes into production. Three are shown in full here: the biggest saving, the riskiest and the quickest. Then all twenty, grouped into the report's four waves.

          Why not switch to DLP?

          The team tested the biggest idea of all (H3) against the report's benchmark. DLP is cheaper to build, but it gives about 1,400 colour lumens against 3,600. In a lit classroom that's a must-be failure, so it was rejected.

          Lamp or laser?

          For the maker, a laser-phosphor engine (H2) costs £27.60 against £14.10 for the lamp and ballast: £13.50 more a projector. It removes lamp changes and lamp warranty claims, and supports a retail premium of £90 or more, so it belongs on a premium model. For the school, it depends on how hard the projector works:

          Gate check before phase 6
          • Savings and one-off costs signed off by finance
          • Every risk has an owner and a test
          • Suppliers have confirmed prices in writing

          6Phase 6 · Presentation

          Ask for a decision, then track the savings

          The study ends with a short meeting and a clear ask, not a long report. The sponsor sees the result, the money, the risks and the plan, and decides. After that, savings are tracked every month against the old cost until they're banked.

          From today's cost to the target

          The plan: 14 months, four waves

          The savings funnel

            After the workshop

            1. Engineer and test each approved idea, with the tests from its business case.
            2. Release it through a formal engineering change, with a date it starts in the factory.
            3. Bank it only when finance sees the lower cost in the actual price paid or the factory's invoice.
            4. Review monthly: savings banked, ideas late, risks that came true. Most savings are lost between approval and the factory, which is why this matters.

            ✓What we learnt

            Eight lessons from one projector

            Facts first, and check them

            A detailed report still had five things that didn't add up. Finding them before the workshop kept every later number honest.

            Module 3 · Information
            Functions, not parts

            The most expensive function, modulating light, was good value. The worst value hid in a cheap one: receiving the signal through ports few schools use.

            Module 4 · Function analysis
            The customer sets the worth

            Over 90% of classrooms use HDMI. That one fact is behind £6.30 of savings: the ports, the smaller board and the cable.

            Module 3 · Information
            How it's made is value too

            £4.05 a projector came from the factory and supply chain (faster curing, shorter burn-in, better stock) with no change to the product.

            Module 9 · Should-costing
            Create first, judge later

            Twenty-four ideas came from keeping the two apart. Three were screened out, each with a written reason.

            Module 5 · Creative
            Agree the criteria before scoring

            Weighted scoring made the choice visible and fair, and it flagged the three ideas that need proving before anyone spends on them.

            Module 6 · Evaluation
            A saving isn't real until it's banked

            Business cases, tests, owners, waves and monthly tracking turn £24.65 of ideas into money in the bank.

            Module 8 · Presentation
            Value depends on who pays

            Eco mode costs the maker nothing and saves the school a quarter of its power. Laser costs the maker more but can suit a busy lecture theatre.
