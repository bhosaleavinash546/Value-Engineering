The classroom projector: a complete value study

One real kind of product, taken apart and through all six phases of a value study. You'll see every part, what it does and roughly what it costs, then watch the method find where the value is. It brings together everything in the course, from function analysis to the business case.

The VH-3600 is a made-up model, built on the usual design of a 3LCD classroom projector. Costs are should-cost estimates built from real volume prices, shown as ranges, with the assumptions and sources at the end of the page.

      B · Bill of materials

      Every part, what it does and what it costs

      The bill of materials is where every value study starts: you can't judge value until you know what's there and what it costs. Each line gives the part's main function as a verb and a noun, ready for function analysis.

      Where the cost sits

      Sub-assemblies by estimated cost, largest first. The top four carry most of the cost, so that's where a study looks first.

        How the estimates were made

        • What a large maker would pay buying parts on the open market: 2025/26 prices, about 500,000 projectors a year, assembled in the Philippines or China, at £1 = $1.30.
        • Built from volume prices, not shop prices: wholesale listings at volume tiers (lamps, prisms, filters, fans, boards, cords), a prism and lens maker's accounts (about $1 per optical part), resin prices and Asian labour rates.
        • Spare parts aren't used. A replacement lamp or panel sells for 5–10 times what the factory pays.
        • Least certain: the LCD panels and the zoom lens. No volume price is published, so they're modelled from wafer and glass costs. A maker that builds its own panels pays less again.
        • Checked from the top down: similar projectors sell for about £375 ex VAT, and a complete Chinese 3LCD projector sells ex-factory for about £250–280 with the assembler's margin in it.

        Sources and price signals

          Listings and shop prices change. Volume listings still carry a trader's margin, so a large maker usually pays 20–50% less than the listed price; the ranges allow for that.
